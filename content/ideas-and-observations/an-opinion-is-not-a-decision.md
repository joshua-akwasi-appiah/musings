---
title: An Opinion Is Not a Decision
description: Why asking for someone’s perspective does not create an obligation to follow it.
---

Earlier today, I made the mistake of asking a friend for her opinion on something. 😅

Well, inasmuch as she did not really share her opinion on the issue I had asked about, she did give me a piece of her mind.

She reminded me of instances when I had asked for her opinion, only to end up doing what I wanted to do anyway. And in that moment, I could not really defend myself, because she was right.

Her question was simple:

> Why do you bother asking me when you know you have already made up your mind about what you are going to do?

The only thing is, I had not made up my mind.

I had an idea, sure. But to say my mind was made up was a different story. An idea is not the same as a decision. Sometimes, you have a direction you are leaning towards, but you still want to hear what someone else sees before you settle on it.

This reminded me of a complaint I have heard in the workplace a number of times:

> Why do you bother asking other people for their opinions when you know you are going to do what you want anyway?

To be honest, I have made a similar complaint before. I think many of us have. Someone asks what we think, we share our view, and then they choose a different direction. The next time they come asking for our opinion, we are tempted to say, “What is the point? You will do what you want anyway.”

As is typical of me, this set my mind on one of its little journeys to unravel the gems in the situation. And here is where I landed: people’s opinions are not supposed to become other people’s decisions.

In its most basic form, an opinion helps someone see a situation from another person’s point of view. It gives them a window into how another person understands the issue, what they may have noticed, what they may be concerned about and what they think matters.

That does not mean one point of view is automatically right and the other is wrong. Most of us believe our point of view makes sense. That is why it is our point of view in the first place.

But when someone comes to me for my opinion, I think my responsibility is to share it honestly. To help them see what I see. To give them the gift of my perspective.

They may take my advice fully. They may take part of it and combine it with other perspectives. Or they may hear me out and still choose a different direction entirely.

That does not make my opinion useless. It means it did what it was supposed to do. It gave them another worldview to consider.

An opinion is not an instruction. It does not transfer the responsibility for a decision from the person making it to the person giving the advice. The person making the decision still has to live with the context, the consequences and the outcome.

This is one reason I value good learning conversations. In learning and development, we can offer people questions, stories, frameworks and perspectives. But we cannot do the learning for them. We cannot decide how they should apply an idea in their own context. Lasting learning requires ownership.

I think opinions work in a similar way.

When someone comes to us for our opinion, let us share it gracefully and deeply, without attaching the expectation that they must do what we have suggested. And if they come back again, even after choosing differently the last time, let us not punish them for it by withholding our perspective.

We have something useful to offer. They have a decision to make. Both things can be true.

And when we are about to make a decision ourselves, it is never wrong to seek other points of view. Asking for someone’s opinion does not mean giving away your agency. It means being open enough to see what you may not have seen on your own.

Have you ever felt frustrated because someone sought your opinion and then chose differently? How did you respond?
